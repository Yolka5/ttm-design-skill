# The brief

Every job starts here, before any code. A first draft built on a guess gets thrown away, and one round of good questions costs less than that. The goal is to understand what the user wants to make well enough that the first result is close.

## 1. Read first

Take in everything the user gave: the request, attached files, linked sites, folders, an existing repo or page. Work out as much as you can yourself. Never ask something the material already answers, and never ask something you could find by looking.

Note what you already know:

- What it is: a page, a section, a whole site, a small tool.
- Who it is for, and what they should do or feel after.
- Which side it is on, Use or Show (see below).
- What content exists: text, data, images, video, brand colours and fonts.
- Where it will live: an artifact, a file, a page in an existing site.

## 2. Ask one round

Ask the questions whose answers would change what you build, all at once, in one message. Use the `AskUserQuestion` tool when it is available: up to four questions, each with two to four answers and your recommended answer first. Without it, write a short numbered list, each question with your suggested answer in brackets, so the user can reply "go with your picks".

Pick from these. Skip any the material already answers.

**Always worth checking**

- **The job.** Is this for people to use or read, or to show a brand, product or person? Ask when the signals point both ways. A "page for our robotics team" could be either.
- **The one outcome.** What should a visitor do or think after? Buy, sign up, trust the company, understand an idea, get a number.

**For Use pages**

- **The answer.** What number or result does the person come for? Which inputs, units and presets?
- **The source of truth.** Where do specs and data come from? Never invent them.
- **The reader.** For lessons and explainers: what do they know already, and how deep should it go?

**For Show pages**

- **The brand.** Name, logo, colours and fonts, or should you propose them? A Show page without a brand comes out generic.
- **The media.** Which images and video exist, and where? Show pages live or die on them.
- **The story.** The three to five things to say, in order, and the one action at the end.
- **Taste.** Sites they like, and what exactly about them. "The buttons", "how the sections swap" and "the dark mood" lead to very different pages.
- **Scope.** One section, one page or the whole site.

**Only when it matters**

- Light, dark or both. Default to both for Use pages and to the brand's own choice for Show pages.
- Phone first or desktop first. Default to both.
- Language and tone of the copy.

## 3. Play it back

Before building, say in three to six short lines what you are going to make: what it is, who it is for, which side, what goes in, and what you are leaving out. This catches a wrong guess while it is still cheap. Then start.

## Keep it short

- **One round, then build.** Don't run a second round of questions before the first draft. Ask about what is left after they see it.
- **Offer choices, not blanks.** "Dark and cinematic, or light and clean?" is easy to answer. "What style do you want?" is not.
- **Don't ask what the skill decides.** Spacing, easing, button styles and type scale are your job.
- **Small jobs get small rounds.** A calculator might need one question, such as which motors and units. That still beats guessing.
- **If the user says to skip questions**, list your assumptions in one line each and start.
- **After the first draft,** ask what to change with specific options, not "any feedback?".
